# Web sources for mapping fields in astronomy and astrophysics

Updated: 2026-05-16

This note collects useful websites that mention different fields within astronomy/astrophysics and provide descriptions of those fields. The sources are prioritized by usefulness for building a serious field map: professional organizations, NASA/JPL pages, university program pages, and educational encyclopedia-style pages.

## Suggested field map synthesized from the sources

The sources above can be reorganized into a practical field map as follows.

### 1. Core astronomy and observational astronomy

Studies celestial objects and phenomena beyond Earth’s atmosphere using observations. This includes planets, moons, stars, galaxies, black holes, and the large-scale universe. Observational work can be carried out across the electromagnetic spectrum, from radio to gamma rays.

Useful sources: Cornell, Smithsonian, AMNH, Britannica, JPL.

### 2. Astrophysics and theoretical astrophysics

Uses physics, mathematics, chemistry, and computation to explain astronomical phenomena. Theoretical astrophysics focuses on modelling, simulation, and solving the physical laws governing systems such as stars, accretion flows, galaxies, compact objects, and the early universe.

Useful sources: Cornell, Tel Aviv University, Britannica Astrophysics, NASA Physics of the Cosmos.

### 3. Cosmology

Studies the universe as a whole, including its origin, evolution, large-scale structure, cosmic microwave background, dark matter, dark energy, and fundamental physics tested through astronomical observations.

Useful sources: Cornell, Britannica, NASA Physics of the Cosmos, NASA Cosmic Origins, IAU.

### 4. Stellar astrophysics

Studies the formation, structure, evolution, and death of stars. Related topics include star clusters, supernovae, stellar populations, nucleosynthesis, and compact remnants.

Useful sources: IAU, NASA Cosmic Origins, University of Michigan, University of Washington.

### 5. Planetary science and exoplanets

Studies planets, moons, asteroids, comets, planetary atmospheres, planetary interiors, and planetary systems inside and outside the Solar System. Exoplanet work includes detection, atmospheric characterization, planet formation, and habitability.

Useful sources: Cornell, NASA Ames, University of Michigan, JPL Researchers by Topic Area.

### 6. Astrobiology

Studies the origin, evolution, distribution, and future of life in the universe. It connects astronomy, planetary science, chemistry, biology, geology, and atmospheric science.

Useful sources: Cornell, IAU, NASA Ames, Space.com astrobiology-related overview.

### 7. High-energy astrophysics and compact objects

Studies energetic phenomena such as black holes, neutron stars, pulsars, magnetars, gamma-ray bursts, relativistic jets, supernova remnants, and accretion. It often connects astrophysics with fundamental physics and relativity.

Useful sources: IAU, NASA Physics of the Cosmos, Tel Aviv University, JPL.

### 8. Gravitational-wave and multimessenger astrophysics

Studies astrophysical systems using gravitational waves and multiple messengers such as electromagnetic radiation, neutrinos, and cosmic rays. Typical sources include binary black holes, binary neutron stars, supernovae, and compact-object mergers.

Useful sources: JPL Researchers by Topic Area, NASA Physics of the Cosmos, IAU.

### 9. Interstellar medium, astrochemistry, and laboratory astrophysics

Studies gas, dust, molecules, ices, and chemical/physical processes in interstellar, circumstellar, cometary, planetary, and lunar environments. Laboratory astrophysics provides experimental and theoretical data needed to interpret astronomical observations.

Useful sources: NASA Ames Astrophysics Branch, NASA Ames Laboratory Astrophysics Capability, JPL.

### 10. Space physics, heliophysics, and Sun–heliosphere studies

Studies the Sun, solar wind, heliosphere, planetary magnetospheres, plasma processes, space weather, and in-situ space measurements. This area overlaps with astrophysics, planetary science, plasma physics, and geophysics.

Useful sources: IAU, JPL Researchers by Topic Area, Cornell Space Sciences.

### 11. Astrostatistics, data science, and AI/ML in astronomy

Develops statistical, computational, and machine-learning methods to analyze astronomical data. This field is increasingly important because modern surveys and missions generate very large datasets.

Useful sources: Cornell, NASA Ames, University of Zurich.

### 12. Instrumentation and observational technology

Designs, builds, and calibrates instruments for ground-based, airborne, and space-based astronomy. This includes telescopes, detectors, spectrographs, high-contrast imaging systems, and mission-support technologies.

Useful sources: Cornell, NASA Ames, JPL.

## Best way to use these sources

For a serious taxonomy, start from Cornell’s list because it gives explicit subfield descriptions. Then compare it with the IAU divisions to make sure the taxonomy reflects professional astronomy. Use NASA/JPL pages to add modern mission-driven and instrumentation-driven fields. Use university pages such as Zurich, Washington, Michigan, and Tel Aviv to understand how these fields appear in actual teaching and research groups. Use Britannica, Smithsonian, AMNH, and Space.com mainly for accessible definitions and introductory explanations.
