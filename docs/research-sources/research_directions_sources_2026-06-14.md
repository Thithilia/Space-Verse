## Authoritative sources used
- USGS: Thermokarst lakes, drainage, and drained basins
  - https://www.usgs.gov/publications/thermokarst-lakes-drainage-and-drained-basins-0
  - Used for the broad framing that lake formation, expansion, drainage, and drained basins are all parts of one lowland permafrost landscape system rather than isolated events.
- USGS: Observing a catastrophic thermokarst lake drainage in northern Alaska
  - https://www.usgs.gov/publications/observing-catastrophic-thermokarst-lake-drainage-northern-alaska
  - Used to support the claim that some lake drainage events are abrupt and can occur over roughly day-scale timing, so sparse imagery should not be overinterpreted without mechanism or field context.
- USGS: Characterizing post-drainage succession in Thermokarst Lake Basins on the Seward Peninsula, Alaska
  - https://www.usgs.gov/publications/characterizing-post-drainage-succession-thermokarst-lake-basins-seward-peninsula
  - Used for the post-drainage framing around vegetation, hydrology, peat, renewed permafrost aggradation, frost heave, and the utility of SAR plus Landsat-type indices.
- NASA ABoVE: Hydrology & Permafrost Working Group
  - https://above.nasa.gov/implementation_plan/hpwg.html
  - Used for the systems framing that thawing permafrost landscapes can drive regional lake and pond drainage, alter vegetation, and shift water storage and transport.
- NASA Earthdata: Arctic Circumpolar Distribution and Soil Carbon of Thermokarst Landscapes
  - https://www.earthdata.nasa.gov/data/catalog/ornl-cloud-thermokarst-circumpolar-map-1332-1
  - Used to justify placing local drained basins inside a broader circumpolar thermokarst and soil-carbon context, without turning the page into a global inventory.
- NASA Earth Observatory: Omulyakhskaya and Khromskaya Bays, Northern Siberia
  - https://science.nasa.gov/earth/earth-observatory/omulyakhskaya-and-khromskaya-bays-northern-siberia-51712/
  - Used for an introductory visual explanation that thermokarst lakes can deepen, connect to lower terrain, and drain, leaving former-lake signatures on the landscape.
- NOAA Arctic Report Card 2021: Glacier and Permafrost Hazards
  - https://arctic.noaa.gov/report-card/report-card-2021/glacier-and-permafrost-hazards/
  - Used to support the hazard/community framing through the Point Lay drinking-water lake drainage example and to keep drainage linked to infrastructure and local services.

## Lab and project-practice sources used
- USGS Alaska Science Center: Alaska Lake Dynamic Studies
  - https://alaska.usgs.gov/portal/project.php?project_id=29
  - Used as an example of how lake drainage research is practiced with multi-scale imagery, in situ sensors, and long time-series analysis.
- University of Alaska Fairbanks: Alaska Geobotany Center
  - https://www.geobotany.uaf.edu/
  - Used only as evidence that drained lake basin systems, thermokarst terrain mapping, vegetation succession, and infrastructure/permafrost interactions are active university research topics.

## Scope and caution notes
- Kept the page explicit that a drained basin is not automatically a stable or hazard-free endpoint; hydrology, carbon response, and local infrastructure implications can still evolve after drainage.
- Avoided quantitative global statements on drainage frequency, methane flux, or permafrost recovery rates because those claims depend strongly on region, basin age, and measurement method.
- Treated the UAF page as evidence of research practice rather than a stand-alone definition of the full subfield.
