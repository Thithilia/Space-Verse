## Authoritative sources used
- NASA Science: Shifting Coastlines in the Arctic
  - https://science.nasa.gov/missions/landsat/shifting-coastlines-in-the-arctic/
  - Used for the broad framing that Arctic coastlines are among the most rapidly changing and that Landsat-scale time series can identify hotspots across 1984-2023.
- NASA Scientific Visualization Studio: Erosion in the Beaufort Sea Coastline
  - https://svs.gsfc.nasa.gov/15023/
  - Used as supporting evidence that the Beaufort Sea coast has a long Landsat-observable erosion record through 2024.
- USGS Coastal and Marine Hazards and Resources Program
  - https://www.usgs.gov/programs/cmhrp/news/permafrost-thaw-and-subsidence-sea-level-rise-and-erosion-are-transforming
  - Used for the coupled framing: thaw, subsidence, sea-level rise, and erosion should be treated together rather than as separate hazards.
- USGS Pacific Coastal and Marine Science Center: Seven Decades of Coastal Change at Barter Island, AK
  - https://www.usgs.gov/centers/pcmsc/news/seven-decades-coastal-change-barter-island-ak
  - Used for the Alaska case-study logic: historical imagery + wave/temperature interpretation + the importance of sea-ice-free season and wave energy.
- NOAA Arctic Report Card 2020: Coastal Permafrost Erosion
  - https://arctic.noaa.gov/report-card/report-card-2020/coastal-permafrost-erosion/
  - Used for the broader circumpolar statement that many monitored permafrost-coast sites show increasing erosion rates since the early 2000s.

## Lab and mission-practice sources used
- NASA ESPO: FORTE
  - https://espo.nasa.gov/forte
  - Used to show the current mission framing that nearshore Arctic research couples rivers, deltas, lagoons, coastlines, and land-ocean fluxes.
- NASA ESSP EVS-4 overview
  - https://essp.nasa.gov/earth-pathfinder-quests/projects/earth-venture-suborbital-evs-4/
  - Used to describe FORTE's multi-platform observing strategy without overclaiming specific results.
- University of Alaska Fairbanks: Arctic Coastal Geoscience Lab
  - https://acgl.community.uaf.edu/
  - Used only as an example of how this research is practiced in Alaska through coastal data collection, hazards work, and community-facing research.

## Scope and caution notes
- Kept the Vietnamese page explicit that Arctic coastal erosion is not reducible to sea-level rise alone; local material, ground ice, waves, open-water season, and subsidence matter.
- Avoided numeric global claims beyond what the cited NASA/USGS/NOAA pages directly supported.
- Treated the UAF lab page as evidence of research practice, not as a stand-alone definition of the whole subfield.

### Authoritative sources used
- NASA: Studies How Arctic Wildfires Change the World
  - https://www.nasa.gov/centers-and-facilities/goddard/nasa-studies-how-arctic-wildfires-change-the-world/
  - Used for the broad framing that Arctic and boreal fires are becoming more frequent/severe and that fire can remove the insulating organic layer protecting permafrost.
- NASA ABoVE: Disturbance Working Group
  - https://above.nasa.gov/implementation_plan/disturbancewg.html
  - Used to frame the subfield as a disturbance research area that explicitly links wildfire to carbon biogeochemistry, permafrost, hydrology, flora/fauna, and ecosystem services.
- NASA: Flights Link Methane Plumes to Tundra Fires in Western Alaska
  - https://www.nasa.gov/earth/nasa-flights-link-methane-plumes-to-tundra-fires-in-western-alaska/
  - Used to support the cautious claim that some burned tundra and wetland settings are associated with stronger methane hotspot occurrence, and that AVIRIS-NG style airborne mapping is part of current practice.
- USGS: Arctic Tundra Fire Causes Widespread Permafrost Landscape Changes
  - https://www.usgs.gov/news/national-news-release/arctic-tundra-fire-causes-widespread-permafrost-landscape-changes
  - Used for the site-specific evidence that repeat airborne LiDAR measured widespread post-fire thermokarst in the Anaktuvuk River fire area.
- USGS EROS: Using Repeat Airborne LiDAR and Landsat to Quantify Arctic Tundra Fire Impacts
  - https://eros.usgs.gov/doi-remote-sensing-activities/2016/usgs/using-repeat-airborne-lidar-and-landsat-quantify-arctic-tundra-fire-impacts
  - Used to justify the methods section on combining long Landsat records with repeat airborne topography for post-fire permafrost change.
- NOAA Arctic Report Card 2024: Arctic Terrestrial Carbon Cycling
  - https://arctic.noaa.gov/report-card/report-card-2024/arctic-terrestrial-carbon-cycling/
  - Used to place wildfire and permafrost change within the broader Arctic carbon-cycle context.

### Lab and network-practice sources used
- Bonanza Creek LTER: Who We Are
  - https://www.lter.uaf.edu/abouts/who_we_are
  - Used as a long-term network example linking wildfire, abrupt thaw, remote sensing, ecosystem response, and community-facing research in Interior Alaska.
- University of Alaska Fairbanks: Northern Ecosystems
  - https://www.uaf.edu/bw/research/northern-ecosystems.php
  - Used only as evidence that fire-permafrost-vegetation dynamics is an active research cluster in current university practice.

### Scope and caution notes
- Kept the page explicit that not every high-latitude fire produces the same permafrost or methane response; burn severity, organic-layer loss, hydrology, vegetation, and permafrost type matter.
- Avoided converting the NASA methane-fire story into a generic statement that wildfire always increases methane everywhere.
- Treated Bonanza Creek LTER and UAF pages as examples of research practice, not as stand-alone definitions of the whole subfield.

### Authoritative sources used
- Drought.gov: Ecological Drought
  - https://www.drought.gov/what-is-drought/ecological-drought
  - Used for the framing that ecological drought is about water deficits pushing ecosystems past vulnerability thresholds, not just low rainfall by itself.
- USGS: Monitoring Vegetation Drought Stress
  - https://www.usgs.gov/special-topics/monitoring-vegetation-drought-stress
  - Used to justify the methods framing around remote-sensing time series, vegetation anomalies, and integrated drought-stress products such as VegDRI.
- NASA ECOSTRESS
  - https://ecostress.jpl.nasa.gov/
  - Used for the claim that canopy/surface temperature and Evaporative Stress Index products can act as early indicators of plant water stress.
- NASA ECOSTRESS: Applications
  - https://ecostress.jpl.nasa.gov/apps
  - Used to support the applied framing that vegetation stress monitoring is connected to wildfire vulnerability, recovery, and management decisions.
- NASA Science: SMAP
  - https://science.nasa.gov/mission/smap/
  - Used for the bridge between drought work and soil-moisture monitoring, with explicit wording that SMAP connects water, carbon, and energy cycles while supporting drought understanding.
- Copernicus European Drought Observatory: Combined Drought Indicator factsheet
  - https://drought.emergency.copernicus.eu/data/factsheets/factsheet_combinedDroughtIndicator_v4.pdf
  - Used for the operational example that drought monitoring can integrate precipitation, soil-moisture anomaly, and FAPAR anomaly rather than rely on a single indicator.

### Lab and program-practice sources used
- CALMIT: Drought research
  - https://calmit.unl.edu/research/drought/
  - Used only as an example of how university remote-sensing groups combine evapotranspiration, soil moisture, vegetation health, and early warning workflows in practice.
- NASA Earth Observatory: Measuring Vegetation (NDVI & EVI)
  - https://science.nasa.gov/earth/earth-observatory/measuring-vegetation-ndvi-evi/
  - Used to keep the page explicit that greenness indices are important but are only one layer of the drought/stress story.

### Scope and caution notes
- Kept the page explicit that drought signals and vegetation/ecosystem stress are related but not interchangeable; the page separates rainfall, soil moisture, canopy stress, and ecological impact.
- Avoided claiming that every vegetation anomaly is caused by drought; heat, land management, fire history, species mix, and phenology can also matter.
- Treated CALMIT as evidence of research practice rather than a stand-alone definition of the entire subfield.
